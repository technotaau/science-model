## p11–12 Waste-to-Energy
- WTE is the process of generating energy (electricity, heat or fuel) from non-recyclable residual waste materials. Dual purpose: reduces garbage going to overflowing landfills and generates power without depleting fossil fuels.
- Under the Clean India Mission, WTE is considered a foundational pillar for building clean, smart cities with minimal carbon footprints.
- Core technologies: thermal or biological.
- INCINERATION (DIRECT COMBUSTION): the most common commercial method. Unprocessed or semi-processed municipal solid waste (MSW) is burned at exceptionally high temperatures (850°C – 1400°C) to boil water. The resulting steam spins a turbine to produce electricity.
- ANAEROBIC DIGESTION (BIOMETHANATION): microbes break down organic wet waste (like kitchen remnants and agricultural runoff) in the absence of oxygen. Yields biogas, which can be purified into Compressed Bio-Gas (CBG) for vehicles or electricity generation.
- CURRENT STATUS OF WTE IN INDIA: India has scaled its focus on converting "Waste to Wealth" through targeted national policies.
  - Capacity Growth: India's overall waste-to-energy capacity has jumped from 230 MWeq in 2014 to 877 MWeq.

## (unnumbered) ODF VILLAGES
ODF stands for Open Defecation Free. An ODF village is a village where people do not defecate in the open and all households have access to and use toilets. It promotes cleanliness, hygiene and a healthy environment. It also reduces the spread of diseases, protects drinking-water sources from contamination and makes the village safer and more comfortable for everyone. Thus, an ODF village is an important step towards a clean, healthy and developed India.

## p14 3 Progressive Stages of an "ODF Plus" Village
An ODF Plus village is one that successfully sustains its ODF status, ensures proper garbage and wastewater management and remains visually clean. Three rungs:
1. ODF Plus Aspiring: sustains zero-open-defecation status and has operational arrangements for either Solid Waste Management (garbage collection/composting) or Liquid Waste Management (greywater drainage/soak pits).
2. ODF Plus Rising: sustains ODF status and has functional infrastructure handling both solid and liquid waste streams.
3. ODF Plus Model: the highest tier. Manages both waste streams, maintains visual cleanliness and actively displays public sanitation awareness messages.

## p15 Green Energy
Green energy is energy produced from natural and renewable sources that cause little pollution. Reduces use of fossil fuels like coal and petroleum; reduces greenhouse gas emissions; conserves natural resources. Examples: solar, wind, hydropower, tidal. Solar panels use sunlight to generate electricity; wind turbines use wind. Useful in homes, schools, farms, industries. Reduces air pollution, protects wildlife and habitats, reduces dependence on non-renewable sources.

## p22 Management of Non-Biodegradable Waste
- Single-use plastics (SUPs): disposable plastic made mainly from petrochemicals (fossil fuels), designed to be used once for a very short time. Examples: grocery bags, straws, coffee stirrers, soda bottles, product packaging.
- ECO-BRICK: a reusable building block made by packing clean, dry, non-recyclable single-use plastic waste tightly into an empty plastic beverage bottle until it is solid and rigid enough to support structural weight. Eco-bricks are crucial to the Clean India Mission because they isolate low-value, non-recyclable single-use plastics right at the source.

## p24–26 Multi-layer gravity filter
- Commonly used as a low-cost, preliminary filtration system. "Stone" = gravel/sand, "cotton" = fine physical barrier, "charcoal" = chemical purifier.
- Excellent for removing large particles and certain chemical impurities, but generally not enough on its own to make industrial chemical wastewater safe for rivers without additional chemical or biological treatments.
- Stones/Gravel/Sand (physical filtration): first line of defence; traps large debris like leaves, twigs, plastics, heavy suspended sediments.
- Charcoal/Activated Carbon (chemical adsorption): the most critical step for chemical water. Millions of microscopic pores trap organic chemicals, chlorine, heavy metals and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) through adsorption (binding molecules to its surface). Also removes bad odours and discoloration.
- Cotton/Filter cloth (fine filtration): final physical barrier; traps tiny particles and charcoal dust.
- Structure: water must flow from the coarsest to the finest material. 1. TOP (inflow): coarse stones and gravel. 2. Second: fine sand. 3. Third: crushed charcoal. 4. BOTTOM: thick layer of cotton or tightly woven cloth.
- Drawing: an upside-down bottle with layers.

## p27–28 How we can keep our rivers clean in Clean India Mission?
We can keep our rivers clean under the Clean India Mission by stopping waste at the source, treating wastewater and involving local communities.
Individual and community actions:
- Stop dumping garbage: never throw household trash, plastic bags or puja offerings directly into rivers or water bodies. Use local dustbins or designated collection systems like 'Puja Banks'.
- Reduce plastic use: cut down on single-use plastics to prevent floating waste reaching river currents and choking aquatic ecosystems.
- Participate in cleanups: join local community river-cleaning drives, awareness campaigns and volunteer programmes near riverbanks and ghats.
- Plant trees: grow local flora and trees along riverbanks to prevent soil erosion, filter runoff water and maintain healthy ecosystems.
Government and industrial measures:
- Treat sewage: build and expand Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs) so untreated human waste and domestic greywater do not flow straight into rivers.
- Regulate industrial waste: enforce strict laws forcing factories to treat toxic chemical effluents before any discharge.
- River surface cleaning: deploy mechanized trash skimmers, boom barriers and conveyor belt systems to collect floating solid waste from major rivers and canals.
